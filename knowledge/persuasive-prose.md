# Persuasive prose — the craft of the sentence

> The locks say **what** the copy has to contain. The anatomy says **in what order**. This file says **how the sentence sounds**, which is what separates a long form the reader finishes from one he abandons at the third block.
>
> Nothing here is decoration. In an 11,000-character piece read on a phone, rhythm is retention.

---

## 1. The rule of three

The highest-yield technique in this file, and the one used on purpose.

The brain processes and remembers in groups of three more easily. Two feels like a coincidence. Four feels like a list. **Three feels like a pattern, and a pattern feels like truth.**

The classic use is the Jobs one: three items, rising rhythm, the third carrying the weight.

**Three ways to use it, in order of force:**

**Symptom triad.** Stacks three concrete consequences, never abstract ones.
> He stopped kissing me at the door. Stopped reaching for me in the morning. Stopped pretending it was temporary.

**Escalation triad.** Each item is worse than the last. The third one is the one that hurts.
> First you take longer. Then you need more. Then you invent an excuse and sleep with your back to her.

**Absolution triad.** Three denials in a row, to lift the blame.
> It is not age. It is not a lack of wanting it. It is not you.

**Where to put it:** the opening (movements 1 to 3), the mechanism beat (8), the close (16). Three triads in a 10k copy is good. Eight is a tic, and the reader feels it.

**The common mistake:** the adjective triad. *Tired, drained and low on energy* is not a triad, it is one synonym said three times. A good triad has three **different facts**.

---

## 2. Sentence length variation

The biggest driver of rhythm, and the most ignored.

A short sentence hits. A long sentence, the kind that stretches and piles clauses and carries the reader down a path without letting him stop, builds tension. Alternating the two is what creates the sense of a human voice.

**The practical rule:** never three sentences in a row at the same length. After two or three long ones, give a three-word one.

> I tried everything. Diet, the gym four times a week, that hundred-dollar booster the guy at the store swore would fix it, ashwagandha on top of all of it, and a whole year waiting for my body to come back on its own.
>
> Nothing came back.

The short cut after the pile-up is where the reader breathes. It is also where he decides to keep going.

---

## 3. The one-line paragraph

On a phone, the paragraph is the unit of reading, not the sentence.

An isolated one-line block works like a drum hit. Use it for:
- the turn of the story
- the sentence the reader will remember
- the conclusion of a movement

> She was not wrong.

Rule: no block longer than four lines in the first 1,500 characters. After that, six at most.

---

## 4. Specificity as proof

A round number sounds invented. A broken number sounds remembered.

*About fifty dollars* is a claim. *Sixty-two dollars* is a memory. The reader does not check; he feels the difference.

Apply it to: price, time of day, years married, lab result, number of attempts, the brand of the product that failed.

> Testosterone at 398. The doctor looked at the sheet for four seconds.

The four seconds are not verifiable, and that is exactly why they work: nobody invents that detail.

---

## 5. Anaphora

Repeating the same opening across consecutive sentences. It creates a hammer and makes reading easier, because the reader already knows the shape and only processes the new content.

> Maybe it caught you at fifty and never let go.
> Maybe you tried the gym, the booster, the appointment.
> Maybe the doctor never even looked up from the chart.

It pairs naturally with the rule of three. Use it in the objection sweep block (movement 16) and in the descent (4).

---

## 6. Antithesis

Two halves in opposition inside the same sentence. It is how you say one thing and deny another at the same time.

> The pill does not give you the man back. It rents him to you for four hours.

> You did not get old. You ran out of passage.

Antithesis is the natural shape of the reframe, which is the central job of movement 10. Every time you have to explain why what he tried failed, try antithesis first.

---

## 7. Asyndeton and polysyndeton

**Asyndeton** is dropping the conjunctions. It accelerates, suffocates, stacks.
> No energy, no drive, no patience, nothing.

**Polysyndeton** is piling them on. It stretches, tires, imitates the weight of time.
> And another month and another year and another excuse.

One accelerates, the other drags. Use the first in the symptom list, the second in the descent.

---

## 8. The sentence that opens a gap

Every transition between movements needs a sentence that forces the next line. Not a cheap cliffhanger; information withheld for exactly one sentence.

> Then he told me the part I was not ready to hear.

> And that was when I understood why nothing had worked.

Rule: **one gap per movement, no more.** A copy full of gaps becomes empty suspense, and the reader feels he is being stalled.

---

## 9. Sensory concreteness

A noun you can see beats an adjective you have to feel.

Bad: *the bedroom felt cold and distant.*
Good: *the sheet on his side was smooth. Not even wrinkled.*

Bad: *I felt humiliated.*
Good: *I waited for her to fall asleep before I got in bed.*

**Do not describe the emotion, describe what produces it.** It holds for the whole piece, and it is what separates copy from a soap opera.

---

## 10. Direct speech without quotation marks

LOCK 11 forbids quotation marks. That does not remove dialogue, it changes the marking. The line goes in its own block, isolated.

> He looked at the labs and said everything was within the normal range.
>
> Within the normal range.
>
> I was sitting there with a man who had not touched me in seven months.

Repeating the line as an echo, in a block of its own, does the job quotation marks would do, and does it better.

---

## 11. Rhythm of revelation

New information arrives one piece per block. Two new facts in the same paragraph and one of them is lost.

In the mechanism movement this is critical: there are three links (cortisol, aromatase, nitric oxide). That is three blocks, not one. And the triad helps:

> Cortisol eats the raw material before it ever becomes testosterone.
>
> Belly fat converts whatever is left into estrogen.
>
> And the same cortisol destroys the nitric oxide, which is what actually opens the vessel.
>
> Three systems. Each one feeding the next.

---

## 12. The plumbing metaphor

Mechanism in physical language, never in biochemistry. The reader has to **see** the problem.

Good ones for this category: a leaking tank, a thief, a pipe closing, a brake left on, a faucet, scar tissue, a toll.

> You were filling a tank with a hole in the bottom. You could pour in as much as you wanted.

One metaphor per copy, sustained to the end. Switching metaphors mid-piece confuses and tires.

---

## 13. The verb carries, the adverb gets in the way

*He left quickly* is weak. *He bolted* is strong.

Cut: very, really, quite, extremely, simply, literally, basically. Each one is a weak verb calling for help.

---

## 14. Second person at the right moment

The copy is in first person (the story), but it turns to second person in three places: the objection sweep, the mirror in the close, and the call to action.

> Maybe you are reading this at two in the morning because you cannot sleep next to her.

The person switch is what turns a story into an offer. Done too early, it breaks the narrative. Done only at the CTA, it arrives late.

---

## 15. The close states, it does not beg

A weak close asks. A strong close presents the choice that already exists.

Weak: *click the link below and find out.*
Strong: *the link is right below. Either you open it, or tomorrow is the same as today.*

It pairs with antithesis and with the triad. It is the last place where all three techniques stack.

---

## Prose checklist (runs alongside the gate)

- [ ] At least **three triads**, and none of them a synonym-adjective triad
- [ ] No run of three sentences at the same length
- [ ] At least one isolated one-line block per major movement
- [ ] Broken numbers, not round ones
- [ ] One central metaphor, sustained start to finish
- [ ] One gap per movement, maximum
- [ ] Emotion shown through behavior, never named
- [ ] Zero intensity adverbs
- [ ] The switch to second person lands at the objection sweep, not before
- [ ] A close that states, instead of begging
